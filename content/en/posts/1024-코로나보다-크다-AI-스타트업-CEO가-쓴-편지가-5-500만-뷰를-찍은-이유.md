If you've ever said "I tried AI and it's not that impressive," you may be in the same position as someone who said "COVID? That's just the flu" in February 2020.

That's at least the claim of one CEO who's been running AI startups for 6 years. On February 9, 2026, Matt Shumer, an AI founder, posted a 5,000-word essay on his blog that exceeded 55 million views on X (formerly Twitter), shaking the world.

Major outlets like Fortune, Inc., and Business Insider immediately covered it, with praise and rebuttals pouring in simultaneously.

**The core message of this essay is just one: "AI change isn't happening 'someday'—it's happening 'now,' and the biggest weapon is quick adaptation."**

**One-liner summary:** In short, AI startup CEO Matt Shumer's viral essay warns that "AI has entered a stage where it creates itself, and 50% of white-collar jobs could be threatened within 1-5 years," but there are substantial counterarguments, so a balanced perspective is needed.

---

## Background

On February 5, 2026, two events occurred simultaneously: OpenAI released GPT-5.3 Codex and Anthropic released Claude Opus 4.6 on the same day. These two models' arrival is being treated as a "turning point" within the AI industry.

Four days later, on February 9, Matt Shumer, co-founder and CEO of OthersideAI (HyperWrite), published an essay titled "Something Big Is Happening."

> Who is Matt Shumer: Co-founder and CEO of OthersideAI, which created the AI writing tool HyperWrite. He majored in entrepreneurship at Syracuse University and founded the medical VR startup Visos in high school. Over 6 years running AI startups, he's also made angel investments in AI infrastructure companies like Cline, OpenRouter, and LiveKit.

There's a reason this essay triggered explosive reactions beyond being a simple industry analysis. Shumer wrote this as **"an honest letter to my family and friends, people unrelated to technology"**—not as "analysis for tech industry people."

Instead of the "polite version" he'd give at cocktail parties, he made public the "crazy but honest version."

There's one more interesting fact. In a subsequent interview, Shumer revealed that he wrote this essay **with Claude's help**.

He said, "I spent hours refining the message with Claude. It helped a lot. And that's exactly my point."

---

## Core Claims of the Essay: "Already Happening to Me"

The logic running through Shumer's essay structure is clear: "Experience, not prediction."

Let's examine the core claims he presents one by one.

### 1. "I am no longer needed for the technical work of my own job"

Shumer describes his Monday work like this: He tells AI, "Make me an app like this. It should work this way, and ideally look like this. Handle the user flow and design yourself." Then AI writes tens of thousands of lines of code.

This was possible before. What's different is what comes next: AI **opens the app itself, clicks buttons, and tests features.**

If something doesn't look right, it fixes and improves itself automatically. Only after meeting its own standards does it report "ready for testing." Shumer says he steps away for 4 hours and returns to find a finished product.

Particularly regarding GPT-5.3 Codex, he uses the words **"judgment"** and **"taste."** It's not simply executing instructions but showing "an intuitive sense of what the right decision is."

### 2. "AI is making itself"

This is the heaviest section of the essay. OpenAI wrote in GPT-5.3 Codex's technical documentation:

> "GPT-5.3 Codex is the first model to play a key role in creating itself. The Codex team used early versions to debug their own training, manage deployment, and diagnose test results and evaluations."

Shumer interprets this as the beginning of an "intelligence explosion."

Each generation of AI creates the next generation, and because that next generation is smarter, it creates the generation after that faster.

Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei also said, "AI is now writing a significant portion of our company's code," and "there may be 1-2 years left until AI autonomously builds the next generation."

### 3. METR Data: Numbers Tell the Story of Speed

The most concrete data Shumer cites is a benchmark from METR (Model Evaluation and Threat Research), a research organization.

METR measures the length of tasks AI can complete independently without human experts. Here, "length" means the time it would take a human expert to perform the same task. The key finding is:

| Time Period | Task Duration AI Can Complete with 50% Probability |
| --- | --- |
| ~1 year ago | ~10 minutes |
| 6 months ago | ~1 hour |
| Recent (Claude Opus 4.5 basis, Nov 2025) | ~5 hours |

These figures are increasing by **2x approximately every 7 months**, and recent data suggests possible acceleration to **2x every 4 months**.

Extending this trend means tasks lasting days become possible within a year, weeks within 2 years, and months within 3 years.

### 4. A Warning About Jobs

Shumer cites Anthropic CEO Amodei's statement: **"AI will replace 50% of entry-level white-collar jobs within 1-5 years."** Shumer himself adds, "Many people in the industry think even this is conservative."

His particular emphasis is that this automation is fundamentally different from before. When factories automated, people moved to offices; when the internet emerged, they moved to logistics. But AI is a **"universal substitute for cognitive labor,"** so there are no empty seats to move to for retraining—that's his logic.

## Rebuttal: Between "Justified Concerns" and "Exaggerated Fears"

With over 55 million views, the rebuttals are equally strong. To maintain balanced perspective, we need to examine the key criticisms.

### Gary Marcus's Critique: "Weaponized Hype"

Gary Marcus, a leading skeptic in AI and professor emeritus at NYU, directly criticized the essay on his Substack as **"weaponized hype stumbling from facts."**

Marcus's core counterargument is this: The METR benchmark Shumer cited uses a **50% success rate**, not 100%.

Furthermore, this benchmark is limited to coding tasks and doesn't measure general tasks.

"No AI can perform all 5-hour tasks that humans can without errors. But reading Shumer's essay, it sounds like they can," Marcus points out.

Marcus also criticized Shumer for not mentioning a separate METR study showing cases where coders gained productivity improvements when using AI but actually experienced productivity drops.

### Fortune's Analysis: "Flawed Assumptions"

Fortune, the day after publishing Shumer's essay, analyzed in a separate article that his logic is flawed.

The core points are:

**Legacy systems and governance barriers.** Those most enthusiastic about AI automation are primarily developers working alone or in AI-native startups. Large organizations have legacy systems, technical debt, and security and compliance issues.

Particularly in regulated industries like finance, law, and healthcare, automation is impossible without establishing result reliability and audit frameworks.

These systems are still in early stages.

### Spyglass's Perspective: "Earthquakes and Volcanoes Are Different"

Another strong counterargument starts from the question: "It's not that AI doesn't matter, but is this specific fear-urgency framing currently justified?" A Spyglass analyst expressed it this way: **"An earthquake doesn't mean you'll be buried in lava."**

He advised, "When an AI startup founder says AI will change everything, remember that in 2021, a crypto exchange founder said the same thing, and in 2016, a VR company founder said it."

## So How Should We Proceed?

Whether Shumer's essay is exaggerated or not, one thing is clear: It's hard to deny that AI capabilities are improving rapidly.

A practical approach is needed that neither gets swept up by hype nor falls behind in change.

Together, let's organize the action guidelines Shumer proposes and a realistic interpretation:

**First, use AI as a work tool, not a search engine.** Shumer recommends "paying $20/month for a paid version of Claude or ChatGPT." The free version is over a year behind the paid version. The key is putting it to actual work, not just asking simple questions. Feed it an entire contract and ask it to find risk clauses, or give it a messy spreadsheet and ask it to build a model.

**Second, experiment with AI for 1 hour daily.** Shumer says, "If you experiment with AI for 1 hour every day for 6 months, you'll understand the situation better than 99% of people around you." The key is direct use, not passively reading AI articles. This advice is reasonable regardless of exaggeration.

**Third, focus on "irreplaceable domains."** Trust and relationships built over long periods, physical on-site work, roles with legal liability, and heavily regulated industries will likely take longer to be replaced by AI. This isn't a permanent shield but buys time.

And as Shumer puts it, "if you spend time denying reality instead of adapting, that time is worthless."

**Fourth, reconsider children's education direction.** The observation that "good grades, good university, stable profession" exactly points to roles most exposed to AI. Rather than optimizing specific career paths, developing curiosity, adaptability, and the ability to create something using AI may be more important.

---

## Conclusion

- Matt Shumer's essay is an honest warning from an AI industry insider to his "family and friends outside tech," conveying a felt sense of the speed with which AI expands from coding to all knowledge work.
- However, we must also consider the limitations of METR benchmarks (coding-specific, 50% success rate), adoption barriers in large organizations, and the stakeholder perspective, and critical thinking to distinguish exaggeration from actual change is necessary.
- Practical tip: Start an AI paid subscription (around $20/month) today, and tomorrow at work, try handing off one task that takes you the most time to AI. If it works, it's time to prepare. If not, try again in 6 months.
