This morning too, I wasted 15 minutes rummaging through my desk looking for a USB drive. I don't remember where I saved the files I organized last week, and I haven't touched my budget tracker for three weeks. I tell myself every Sunday that I'll definitely organize this weekend, but when the weekend comes, I'm overwhelmed by where to start.

Does this sound familiar?

## The Real Reason We Fail at Organization

I've read books about organizing and searched for YouTube videos, but there's a reason for the three-day rule. It's because **we approach knowledge organization, space organization, and budget organization with completely different methods**.

Monday we install a new file organization app, Tuesday we watch minimalist lifestyle videos and reorganize our closet, Wednesday we download a new budget app. Eventually, trying to run too many systems simultaneously leads to abandoning all of them.

But after 10 years of organization consulting, I've discovered something: **all organization follows common principles**. I call this the "integrated organization system," and once you understand it, you can apply the same approach to any area.

## Part 1. Three Core Rules You Must Follow

### Rule 1. Category Classification - "Remember the Rule of 3"

Organization starts with classification. But if you divide things too finely, management becomes difficult. The method I recommend is the **'3-3-3 Rule'**: divide everything into 3 major categories, then divide each into 3 sub-categories, and if necessary, further divide into 3 minor categories.

For example, if organizing knowledge, divide it into 'In Progress', 'Reference', 'Archive'. Then divide 'In Progress' again into 'Today's Tasks', 'This Week's Tasks', 'This Month's Tasks'. This way, any material will definitely belong to one of 9 categories.

Physical items work the same way. Divide them into 'Use Daily', 'Use Sometimes', 'Store'. It was difficult because we were trying to organize a toothbrush used daily by the same standard as a travel suitcase used once a year.

What about your budget? Divide it into 'Must-Spend Money' (fixed costs), 'Adjustable Money' (variable costs), 'Future Money' (savings/investment). Suddenly it becomes clearer. You no longer need to struggle with the same reasoning for rent and a coffee.

### Rule 2. Immediate Processing - "The Magic of 2 Minutes"

David Allen, creator of GTD (Getting Things Done), said: "If you can finish something in 2 minutes, do it immediately." Applying this principle to organization creates surprising changes.

If you read an email, move it to a folder immediately. 30 seconds is enough. If you use a pen, put it back in the pen holder right away. It takes 1 second. If you make a card payment, enter it in your smartphone budget app right there. 1 minute is sufficient.

"Won't it be more efficient to do it all at once later?" you might think. Actual measurements show the opposite. It takes 30 minutes to organize a week's worth of receipts in bulk, but entering them daily takes only 7 minutes total (1 minute × 7 days). Plus, nothing gets missed and you avoid the stress of trying to reconstruct memories.

In my experience, practicing this '2-minute rule' for just 3 weeks becomes a habit. Initially, you have to do it consciously, but soon your hands move automatically. Like automatically placing your cup in the sink after coffee.

### Rule 3. Regular Review - "Trust the System, But Verify It"

No system, no matter how good, survives without review. But you can't do spring cleaning every day. So I use the **'5-15-30 Review Method'**.

Every day 5 minutes: End your day by resetting your desk. Put today's files in their proper place and prepare what you need for tomorrow. Like washing dishes after cooking—it's time to tidy up the day's traces.

Every week 15 minutes: Review the week and check your system. Organize emails accumulated this week, empty the downloads folder, review this week's spending. This is when you ask "Why isn't this in its place?" to find system gaps.

Every month 30 minutes: Close out the month and find improvements. What was the messiest place this month? What item did I search for most? Which budget categories went over? Through these questions, you evolve your system.

## Part 2. Real-World Rules for Maximum Efficiency

### Rule 4. Labeling - "What Has No Name Does Not Exist"

I once organized an office of a major company executive. Their secret was simple: **everything had labels**. Each drawer, each file, even each USB had clear name tags.

For digital files, I recommend the format 'Date_Project Name_Version'. For example, '240315_Monthly Report_Final'. This way, files automatically sort by date and are easy to find in searches.

In physical spaces, it needs to be more intuitive. Rather than writing 'Cables,' write 'Charging Cables - iPhone, Galaxy, Laptop' specifically. So even the future you can understand at a glance after 6 months.

### Rule 5. The 80/20 Rule - "Don't Manage Everything Equally"

Pareto's Law applies to organization too. Only 20% of all items are actually used frequently. Similarly, about 20% of files are regularly referenced, and about 20% of budget items account for 80% of spending.

The key is finding this 20% and managing it specially. Keep pens and scissors you use daily in a desk pen holder; put a stapler you use once a year deep in a drawer. Put frequently referenced work manuals on your desktop; store contracts you check monthly deep in cloud storage.

I do a 'usage frequency audit' every 3 months. I re-evaluate what's actually used frequently and adjust locations. Surprisingly, when seasons change, the items you frequently use change too.

### Rule 6. Single Location Storage - "Truth Is One"

Many people scatter important information across multiple locations. Contact information is in your phone, notebook, and Excel. Then every time you update, you have to modify all locations, and eventually, it becomes unclear which is latest.

Apply the **'Single Source of Truth'** principle. Store all passwords in only one password manager, all schedules in only one calendar, all notes in only one note app.

The same goes for physical items. If pens are scattered throughout your house, they're hard to find when you need them. Gather all writing instruments in one place and only keep the minimum where needed. After use, always return them to 'headquarters.'

## Part 3. Building an Organization System

### First Week: Understanding Current State and Creating Classification System

Before starting to organize, you need to understand the current situation. Take a sheet of paper and draw three circles. Each represents knowledge, space, and finances. Inside each circle, write what currently causes you the most stress.

For example, in the knowledge circle, you might write "Overwhelming daily emails," "Disorganized work files," "Unread materials." Naturally, priorities become visible.

Now create a 3-3-3 classification system for each area. Don't try to make it perfect. 70% correct is sufficient. You'll continuously improve as you actually use it.

### Real-World Case Study: Manager Kim's Transformation

Let me introduce the case of Marketing Manager Kim. He wasted over 30 minutes daily searching for materials and even missed an important meeting because he couldn't find his presentation.

First, I divided work files into 3 folders: 'In Progress/Complete/Reference'. In Progress was further divided into 'This Week/This Month/Waiting', and Complete was divided into '2024/2023/Earlier'.

Every day before leaving, he spent 5 minutes organizing files from today's work. Every Friday afternoon, he spent 15 minutes organizing the week and moving items from 'In Progress' to 'Complete'.

The results were remarkable.

Within 3 weeks, file search time dropped to under 5 minutes. After 2 months, he received the question: "Manager Kim, how do you organize your files so well?"

## Closing: Small Success Creates Big Change

Organization isn't completed overnight. But if you practice just one of the 10 rules introduced today, you'll notice clear changes within a week.

**Choose one thing you can start today.**

I would start with the '2-minute rule.' Right where you're reading this, pick up one item you see in front of you and put it back in its place.

It takes only 2 minutes, but that's where change begins.

**✨ The purpose of organization isn't to create a perfect space, but to create room to focus on what matters.**
