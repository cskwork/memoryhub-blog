"Starting today, we're implementing a new performance evaluation system."

CEO Choi's voice rings out before all gathered employees. His voice is cold and resolute. About a hundred employees sitting in the squirrel-cage-like conference room don't utter a sound.

"This is an unavoidable measure for company survival. The 'Performance Survival Project' beginning this quarter will evaluate performance by department, team, and individual in real-time, systematically eliminating the bottom 10%."

I feel my colleague Minseok's face go pale beside me. Manager Kim from Sales across the room bites his lip.

"It spans 6 months with 3 stages total. 10% of all employees are eliminated at each stage. Survivors receive substantial bonuses and promotion opportunities."

The moment the CEO finishes, the conference room freezes before filling with whispers. Some faces redden with anger, others pale with fear. A few already send threatening glances to neighboring colleagues.

"If there are no more questions, I'll relay details through department heads. Meeting dismissed."

As the CEO exits, the conference room erupts into chaos. I'm a 15-year veteran director, and this isn't my first restructuring—I experienced one during the 2008 financial crisis and again during COVID. But never this publicly cruel.

"Director Jinwoo, is this real?" Minseok asks with trembling voice.

"Feels like a game, right? But this is real. Winner-takes-all world," I answer coolly.

But despite my calm tone, my heart races. I have a stroke-stricken father and a middle-school daughter. After my divorce, I'm raising her alone—this job is my lifeline.

---

The first stage is inter-team competition. Teams A and B performing identical work compete, with lower-performing team's underperformers automatically eliminated. Our Marketing Team 2 must compete against Marketing Team 1.

A new "Survival Board" appears in the company system. Real-time performance scores display for each team and individual. Like an online game ranking system.

Colleagues who previously laughed while having coffee now eye each other warily. Team meetings become fiercely competitive over whose ideas get adopted. Overtime becomes routine, weekend work expected.

"Director, what do you think of the project proposal I prepared?" Minseok asks.

He's my closest junior, entering the company together six years ago. His proposal has some flaws, but the core idea is good.

"It's decent, but market analysis is weak. Need more concrete data."

Minseok thanks me and returns to his seat. But I don't tell him something: it's too similar to my in-progress project. Submitting first matters.

That night, I stay late, strengthening my proposal. I reinforce exactly the parts I advised him on. Early the next morning, I submit to my team lead.

When Minseok arrives with his proposal, betrayal flashes in his eyes. I feel momentary guilt, then steel myself. "This is a game. Survival is the objective."

---

When stage one ends, we barely survive. 2% higher performance than Marketing Team 1. But three from our team are eliminated. One is Lee from Planning, two months from her wedding. Everyone stays silent watching her pack.

Stage two is individual competition. Now teammates must watch each other. Individual targets are set, achievement rates updating in real-time on the Survival Board.

The company atmosphere grows frigid. Information sharing and collaboration—once natural—disappear. Everyone focuses solely on their numbers. Some even sabotage teammates.

"Director Seo at strategic planning mentions the boss's daughter's wedding is Friday. You attending?" an office coffee machine ask.

I hesitate. The boss is my supervisor, but Friday is a critical project deadline.

"Of course. We should congratulate him."

"I see. I have a deadline so... too bad," my colleague's eyes calculate—he thinks I can't focus on the project.

That night, I cancel my daughter's promised movie and work through the night. Friday morning, I submit the project, then rush to the wedding. My colleague's shocked expression stays with me.

---

Stage three identifies the final survivor. Remaining staff: 80% of original. Another 10% eliminated. The cruelty: peer evaluation. Colleagues rate each other.

"By Friday, name three colleagues you don't want to work with. Include reasons."

The HR announcement plunges the office into gloom. Colleagues avoid eye contact. Everyone desperately tries to appear favorable, hoping not to be named.

At lunch, eating alone in the cafeteria, Minseok sits across from me.

"Eating alone, Director?"

His tone is friendly, but his eyes are different. Since stage one, we've grown distant.

"Yeah, complex times."

"Director, can I be honest? I'm thinking of writing your name."

I put down my spoon, shocked by his bluntness.

"Why?"

"I know you copied my idea that day. The team lead told me."

His face smiles, but his eyes shine coldly.

"Minseok, that was—"

"It's fine. I understand. Everyone needs to survive. Me too."

He stands up. My chest feels heavy. I never wanted this. We were once genuine colleagues.

Friday approaches. I struggle all night. Whose names do I write? Who gets eliminated? My mind churns in confusion.

That morning, instead of the office, I stand before the CEO's office. The secretary eyes me curiously as I knock.

"Come in."

CEO Choi looks surprised as I enter.

"Director Kim Jinwoo. What brings you?"

"CEO, I want to discuss the 'Survival Project.'"

"Oh, you're in the top 10% now. No need to worry."

"It's not that. This project is destroying our company."

The CEO's eyes turn cold.

"Explain."

"Three months ago, we helped each other grow together. Now? Everyone's an enemy. Information doesn't flow. Collaboration's gone. How can innovation emerge from this?"

The CEO sits silent for a long time, then slowly speaks.

"Director Kim, coming here took courage. But the world is harsh. Only the strong survive."

"Then our company faces the same fate. True innovation is impossible where everyone thinks only of themselves."

Our conversation stretches nearly 30 minutes. Finally, I state my decision.

"I won't participate further in this project. If necessary, I'll resign."

The CEO's expression grows complex. After thinking, he says:

"Director Kim, I want to hear more. This evening at 7 PM, my office?"

---

That evening, the CEO's office hosts several mid-level employees from various departments. We share experiences and thoughts. Most mention the "Survival Project's" negative effects.

A week later, company-wide email arrives:

"The 'Performance Survival Project' ends today. Instead, we're implementing a new collaboration-focused evaluation system. Details forthcoming."

The office fills with mixed chaos and relief. Many approach me expressing gratitude. Including Minseok.

"Director, thank you for your courage. I... should apologize."

I smile. "We were all just pawns in a game. Now we need to relearn how to work together."

That evening, I finally take my daughter to the movies. It feels like releasing a heavy burden. I realize: survival matters, but losing humanity in the process isn't true survival.

Walking back to work, I decide never to be a game participant again. Sometimes questioning the game itself matters more than playing.

The End.
