It was raining. The June rainy season matched my mood exactly, unjustly. The day Samsung's personality test results are announced. Since morning, sweat has been seeping into my palm gripping the phone. My heart pounds every time I refresh my email.

"Kim Junho, unfortunately..."

I don't need to read further. After three years, I've memorized the pattern of these sentences. Unfortunately, regrettably, sadly... Are their regrets genuine? How much can they truly understand my life's regret?

I put the phone down. In front of Gangnam Station subway entrance, I'm getting rained on without even properly holding an umbrella. Passing people have varied expressions. Busy businessmen rushing, sleepy college students heading to school, and faceless people like me. Are they job seekers too?

28 years old, graduated from a provincial national university with a business degree, TOEIC 920, computer proficiency level 1, various certifications and volunteer work, intern experience... My credentials are slightly above average for job seekers nationwide. I'm above average, so why is there no passing result?

---

"Junho, how did it go this time?"

A call from my mother. Her voice carries tension. I didn't even want to imagine how my parents feel waiting for each of my interview results anymore.

"I'm sorry, Mother. This time too..."

A brief silence follows. It's surprisingly heavy.

"It's okay, son. You'll have other chances. Didn't you hear that the son next door from the Choi family got into Samsung after retaking the exam? You can do it too."

It was meant to comfort me, but somehow it hurt more. The comparison with "the Choi family's son next door" has continued for three years. He from Kookmin University has always been my comparison benchmark. My parents meant no malice. Their words came from wanting their son to succeed. But those words have been slowly cracking my self-esteem.

"Yes, Mother. Don't worry. I'm eating well."

After hanging up, I turn my steps toward a café in Gangnam. The place, known as a mecca for large company job seekers, is packed as expected with people working on laptops. On their screens are cover letter templates, exam prep problem sets, and blind hiring information sites. Everyone dreams the same dream, running toward the same goal.

I sit down and order an Americano. 5,500 won. Not a small amount for a job seeker, but this is my rental fee for spending the whole day in this space. I open my laptop and automatically pull up JobKorea and Saram-in sites. The job posting page is my browser's home page.

"Junho!"

I turn my head at the voice from behind. It's Lee Minwoo, my college classmate. He got a job at a small company two years ago. Even though it's Saturday, he's dressed in formal attire.

"Hey, long time no see. What are you doing here?"

"What else, preparing for jobs. You're coming in at this hour?"

Minwoo sits down with a sigh.

"Coming back from a client meeting. I'm going crazy working even on weekends."

His face doesn't have that bright expression from college days. Seeing him, I feel some relief thinking 'so that's how life is even after getting a job,' but soon I'm ashamed of my own thought.

"How have you been? Still chasing Samsung?"

"I got rejected again today."

"Ah, really? I'm sorry. But why do you keep insisting on just that company? There are plenty of good small companies. Our company is even hiring..."

Looking at Minwoo trailing off, I knew his offer was genuine. But a small company? My parents' expectations, three years of effort, and the practical salary gap... I couldn't readily agree.

"Thanks Minwoo. But honestly... I want to challenge myself a bit more."

Minwoo doesn't say another word. He just silently drinks his coffee. In college, I had better grades and more leadership. But now? He has a job, a salary, and the title of "working adult." Comparisons are always cruel.

---

Evening comes and I meet Soyeon. She's now a junior manager at K Bank, someone I met at a job preparation study group two years ago. Our relationship is peculiar. She mostly pays for our dates. When I pay, she doesn't show it, but her consideration for my financial situation is evident.

"Didn't work out again today."

Soyeon says this as soon as she sees my expression. I nod. No further explanation is needed.

"Junho, don't lose heart. You're a good person."

Her comfort actually makes me feel smaller. A good person? What does that even mean? Society doesn't want "good people." It only wants people with good credentials, abilities, and skills relevant to specific positions.

"Soyeon, sometimes I think. What am I living for?"

This words come out suddenly during dinner. I wouldn't normally say this.

"What do you mean?"

"Was getting into a large company really my life's final goal? Or am I just following along because everyone else wants it?"

Soyeon puts down her chopsticks and looks serious.

"Junho, I'll be honest. You seem to be losing confidence lately. Getting a job really does require luck too. But that doesn't mean you should change your life's direction. Large companies don't just provide money—they guarantee stability and a future."

She's not wrong. But something inside me starts to bubble up.

"But I'm spending the last few years of my twenties for that stability. For three years, I've been sitting at the same desk, writing the same cover letters, dreaming the same dreams."

Soyeon's expression hardens.

"Junho, so what's the alternative? Are you just going to give up? I don't want to say this, but your situation is difficult. A 28-year-old provincial university graduate... if you give up now..."

I look at Soyeon who stops speaking. Cold reality recognition from the person I've loved. I can't say she's wrong. But her words leave a deep wound in my heart.

"I'm sorry, I didn't mean it that way."

"No, you're right. I need to face reality."

After dinner, we exchange awkward farewells. On the train back, looking out the window, I think. The gap between Soyeon and me isn't just about employment. We're living in different worlds.

---

I return to my goshitel and throw myself on the bed. The 3-pyeong space is cramped, but for 350,000 won monthly rent, it's the best I can afford in central Seoul. Beyond the wall, I hear someone's snoring, the sound of turning pages, keyboard typing. How many dreams and despairs coexist in these tiny spaces?

Piles of self-help books stack up on the desk next to my bed. "NCS Practice Problems in One Day," "Interview Secrets from Successful Candidates," "Cover Letter Writing Tips from HR Managers"... books I've collected over three years. And next to them, a laptop containing a cover letter file revised over 100 times.

I pick up my phone and open Instagram. My feed is full of friends' office lives, overseas trips, newlyweds' daily routines. As a job seeker, I don't even have content to post on SNS. My last post was from a year ago—a photo from Samsung's intern completion ceremony. Back then, I dreamed of converting to permanent employment. But that door was too narrow.

Outside the window, city lights sparkle. Seoul doesn't sleep. Employees working overtime, students studying, and young people like me worrying about the future... we're all fighting our own battles.

I put the phone down and open the memo app. My job search journal for three years. Interview reviews, thoughts, areas for improvement—all written down. As I try to write about today's interview, different words suddenly flow from my fingertips.

"What am I living for?"

This question is sudden, but in fact, it's been lodged in a corner of my heart for a long time. Was getting into a large company really my dream? Or was it just a goal to meet society's and my parents' expectations?

Choosing business administration, starting TOEIC prep—all these choices were made under the name of a "stable future." But now, I'm living not in stability but in endless anxiety.

Sitting by the window, I think. What did I genuinely enjoy during college? It was when I ran a small business marketing project in my departmental club. Creating an online marketing strategy for a small furniture workshop and watching their sales actually increase—that exhilaration. Back then, I thought that wasn't "real work." I believed I needed to go to places like Samsung, Hyundai, LG to truly succeed.

That night, after a long time, I fall into deep thought. And suddenly, I realize. What I want isn't a large corporation's business card. What I want is to do something meaningful. To use my abilities to genuinely help someone.

---

The next morning, I wake up earlier than usual. Strangely, my heart feels light. I decide to try something different starting today. First, I contact a senior from my university club. He runs a small marketing consulting company.

"Junho, long time no see. How have you been?"

"Senior, honestly, it hasn't been easy. Three years of just chasing large corporations..."

"I see. I heard you interned at Samsung too? Didn't you get converted to permanent?"

"No..."

A brief silence. Then, gathering courage, I speak.

"Senior, is there any work I could help with? I'd be happy to do it without pay. I just want to participate in actual projects."

The senior thinks for a moment, then answers.

"Perfect timing. We're starting one SME support project, but we're short on staff. Interested?"

That afternoon, I find the senior's small office. Far from the flashy large corporation buildings of Gangnam. In a converted officetels space, there are five employees including the senior. They help local SMEs with digital marketing.

"This project is a 40-year-old traditional handmade shoe workshop. The craftsmanship is great, but they know nothing about online marketing. I heard you did a similar project in your club. Got any ideas?"

At the senior's question, my mind suddenly comes alive. The project I did in college comes to mind. The passion, creativity, and sense of accomplishment back then...

"Yes, I've got a few ideas!"

For the first time, speaking actual business ideas instead of cover letters, my voice overflows with energy. This isn't something I'm doing because someone told me to. This is work I genuinely want to do.

I participate in the project for two weeks. There's no pay, but the sense of accomplishment is priceless. Most of all, the moment when the workshop owner smiles brightly seeing the ideas I proposed—that moment is worth more than any acceptance letter.

"Junho, you have more talent for this kind of work than I thought."

On the day we finish the project, the senior says this.

"Actually, we're hiring one more person soon. Interested? The salary isn't as much as large corporations. But you'd have plenty of opportunities to lead projects directly."

For a moment, I hesitate. The salary would be less than half what large corporations offer. How will I explain to my parents? What will Soyeon think? But I can't give up the vitality and meaning I felt over these past two weeks.

"Thank you, senior. I'm truly interested."

---

A month later, I'm sitting in Hangang Park. Now I'm an employee at a small marketing company. When I went home to explain to my parents, my father unexpectedly listened to me seriously.

"Son, if you're happy, that's what matters. My generation didn't have much choice. But your generation is different."

My mother was worried at first, but after seeing energy return to my face for the first time in a while, she gradually came to accept it.

Soyeon and I... eventually broke up. She couldn't understand my choice, and I couldn't change her values. It hurts, but it was the better decision for both of us.

Looking at the Han River's flowing water, I think. My life didn't suddenly become perfect. I still live in a cramped goshitel, and my salary isn't generous. But at least every morning when I wake up, I want to go to work. I know the work I do genuinely helps someone.

Rain starts to fall. This time, I have an umbrella prepared. Is life like this too? The process of preparing, failing, and rising again. I've realized that credentials aren't the only umbrella that shields life's rain.

I take out my phone and open Instagram, which I've neglected for so long. I post a new photo.

"A new beginning. Looking for something beyond credentials."

The photo is my new company business card and a small potted plant. The company atmosphere allows me to freely design my own business card, and on it is written "Kim Junho, Project Manager." The plant was a gift from my senior on my first day. It came with a message: "I want to see your growth."

Walking in the rain, this time it doesn't feel sad. Rather, it feels like being washed clean and starting fresh. Tomorrow is another SME project meeting. Listening to their stories, achieving their dreams together. That's what I truly wanted—something "beyond credentials."

They say dreams exist to be realized, not just dreamed. But perhaps confirming whether that dream is truly your own comes first.
