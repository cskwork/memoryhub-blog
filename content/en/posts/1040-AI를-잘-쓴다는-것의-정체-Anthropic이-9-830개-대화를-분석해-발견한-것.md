More people use AI every day, but when asked "are you using it well?" most lack confidence. There's an even bigger problem.

The more convincing AI's output appears, the less critically people examine it. Beautifully packaged code, neatly organized documents. The moment something seems polished, critical thinking switches off. This paradox that Anthropic uncovered by analyzing about 10,000 actual conversations directly confronts the question of what real competency is needed in the AI age. **People who use AI well are those who don't treat AI's first answer as the final result.**

**One-liner summary:** In short, the core of AI fluency is in "iteratively refining conversations" and "being more skeptical of increasingly polished results."

---

## Background

In February 2026, Anthropic published a research report titled 'AI Fluency Index'.

It analyzed 9,830 actual conversations from Claude.ai to quantitatively measure how effectively people collaborate with AI.

What's interesting is the starting point of this research. It wasn't about "how much AI do you use?" but "how do you use AI?"

This study began from the concern that adoption rates alone don't reveal actual effectiveness.

> AI Fluency refers to the ability to interact with AI systems in ways that are effective, efficient, ethical, and safe.

This definition comes from the **4D AI Fluency Framework** developed by Professor Rick Dakan (Ringling College of Art and Design) and Professor Joseph Feller (University College Cork) in collaboration with Anthropic.

It goes beyond simple technical prompt-writing skills to encompass a competency system covering the entire AI collaboration.

The four core competencies of the 4D Framework are:

| Competency | English | Key Question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Delegation | Delegation | Which tasks should I delegate to AI, and which should remain with humans? |
| Description | Description | How clearly can I communicate what I want to the AI? |
| Discernment | Discernment | Can I accurately evaluate AI's output? |
| Diligence | Diligence | Do I take responsibility for the final product and maintain ethical standards? |

These four can be compared to cooking. Delegation is deciding "blend this ingredient in a mixer, but hand-cut that one."

Description is conveying the recipe precisely. Discernment is tasting the finished dish to check the seasoning.

Diligence is the final checkpoint before serving to guests: "Is this food safe and honest?"

Anthropic selected 11 of these 24 behavioral indicators that could be directly observed in Claude.ai conversations.

The remaining 13 (honest disclosure of AI use, consideration of consequences in sharing AI-generated content, etc.) are behaviors that happen outside the conversation, so

they couldn't be measured in this study.

## Key Finding 1: "Iterative Refinement" is the Key to All Competencies

The strongest pattern in the research was the correlation between **iteration and refinement** and all other AI usage competencies.

85.7% of analyzed conversations showed iteration and refinement. These were conversations where users didn't simply accept AI's first response but asked follow-up questions or adjusted direction to develop the output.

The key numbers make this difference clear. Conversations with iterative refinement (8,424 conversations) showed an average of 2.67 additional AI usage competency behaviors.

Conversations without iteration (1,406 conversations) averaged only 1.33. Almost exactly double the difference.

Particularly notable is the gap in evaluation-related competencies. The probability of users questioning AI's reasoning in iterative conversations was 5.6 times higher than in non-iterative ones. The probability of spotting missing context showed a 4x difference.

This means something clear. There's a qualitative difference in usage competency between people who end an AI conversation in one question-answer exchange and those who refine through multiple back-and-forths.

It's like the difference between submitting a first draft and editing it multiple times. The first answer is a starting point, not the final result.

## Key Finding 2: The More Polished the Output, the Less Critical Thinking

The second finding contains a more uncomfortable truth.

12.3% of all conversations included artifacts.

Here, artifacts mean concrete outputs generated by AI like code, documents, or interactive tools.

In conversations containing artifacts, users were **significantly more likely to clarify goals** (+14.7%p), **specify format** (+14.5%p), and **provide examples** (+13.4%p). In other words, they were more actively directing the AI at the early stages of conversation.

But a paradoxical phenomenon emerged. While instructions became more specific, actual verification of the output decreased.

| Behavioral Indicator | Change vs. Non-Artifact Conversations |
| --- | --- |
| Missing Context Identification | -5.2%p decrease |
| Fact Checking | -3.7%p decrease |
| Questioning AI Reasoning | -3.1%p decrease |

Simply put, when AI produces clean code or attractive documents, people tend to say "looks good" and move on.

Much like how pretty plating at high-end restaurants makes us less critical of taste,

**highly polished appearances are disarming critical evaluation in AI usage too**.

Anthropic stated that multiple explanations are possible for this pattern. One is that when AI's output looks complete, people may feel additional verification is unnecessary. Another possibility is that work involving artifacts might be the kind where aesthetic elements or functionality are more important (like UI design).

It's possible users are verifying outside the conversation, like by running code themselves or showing it to colleagues.

Regardless of which explanation is correct, the report's core point remains: In an era where AI produces increasingly convincing outputs,

the value of the ability to critically evaluate those outputs doesn't diminish—it actually increases.

## Practice: Three Ways to Improve AI Usage Competency

These concrete practices are derived from data in the Anthropic report.

**① Don't Stop at the First Answer**

Iteration and refinement showed the strongest correlation with all other AI usage competencies. When receiving AI's response, you can try these follow-up actions:

- Ask "Why did you make that decision?" to understand the reasoning process
- Confirm "Is anything missing?" to identify missing context
- Request "Are there alternative approaches?" to explore other options

The key is treating AI's first response as a draft.

**② Be More Skeptical of Highly Polished Results**

When AI-generated code runs without errors or documents come back neatly organized, that's precisely when more verification is needed.

- For generated code: Are edge cases handled? Are there performance issues?
- For generated documents: Are the facts accurate? Are there missing perspectives?
- For generated analysis: Are the premises correct? Are there data interpretation errors?

It's important to consciously maintain skepticism toward results that look good.

**③ Set Conversation Rules First**

According to the research, only 30% of users specified interaction methods to AI.

Pre-communicating guidelines like these at the start of conversation changes collaboration quality:

- "Flag if my assumptions are wrong"
- "Show me your reasoning before giving me the answer"
- "Mark parts you're uncertain about"

This pre-setting transforms AI conversation from one-way instruction to two-way collaboration.

## Conclusion

- Anthropic's analysis of 9,830 actual conversations revealed that AI competency's core lies in iterative refinement patterns. Conversations showing this pattern demonstrated 2x more competency behaviors than those without it.
- A paradox was discovered where the more polished AI's output appears, the less critically users evaluate it. Fact-checking, questioning reasoning, and identifying missing context all decreased.
- Practical tip: Today, after asking AI a question, try asking just one follow-up: "Why did you make that decision?" as your starting point.
